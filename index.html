Proposed doctoral research

Health information equity in generative AI: how language shapes information completeness and user trust

Computational audit · Behavioral experiment

Does generative AI provide equally complete health information in English, Russian, and Kyrgyz? When important details are missing, can users recognize that, and do they still trust the answer? At the center is trust calibration: does trust follow the completeness of an answer, or the confidence of its wording?

AI is entering healthcare, one of the most important parts of people’s lives, and about one in five US residents speak a language other than English at home (U.S. Census Bureau, 2023). Existing research compares AI health answers across languages: Jin and colleagues (2024) evaluated English, Spanish, Chinese, and Hindi, and Adilmetova and colleagues (2025) studied nutrition advice in English, Russian, and Kazakh. These studies examine the answers, not how readers respond to them. This project connects the two, focusing on English, Russian, and Kyrgyz speakers in the United States, whose trilingual, culturally shaped information habits differ from those of speakers in Kyrgyzstan.

The project has two parts. The first is computational: an audit of about 100 AI responses in English, Russian, and Kyrgyz, scored against clinician-informed answer keys to measure how complete each answer is. The second is a human-centered, preregistered experiment with adults in the Chicago area, testing whether readers detect missing information and how it affects their trust. Together, they show whether readers’ trust tracks what an answer actually contains.

My first health-information study took place in my mother’s hospital in Ibb, Yemen, where I built a website for readers whose digital literacy and trust had yet to be built. Analyzing mental-health content for Medicinal Media at Boston University, and later leading UX research on four health projects at The Vyater Group, I began asking what the real social impact of this work is beyond the numbers. Inspired by my mother’s care for people in need, I want to help underserved communities recognize incomplete or misleading health information.

Writing sample

Understanding Effective Digital Narratives for Mental Health Branding Among Youth: A Case Study of Medicinal Media

COM EM 777 Masters Collaboratory Project · Advised by Dr. Betsi Grabe and Adam Moitoso · June 2025

How can Medicinal Media, a nonprofit that promotes mental wellness through storytelling, better resonate with Gen Z on social media? The study is guided by brand personality and brand congruity theories.

A quantitative analysis of engagement metrics (likes, views, retention) across all of the organization’s Instagram posts and YouTube videos, with NRC Emotion Lexicon sentiment analysis in R to measure emotional tone.

The content was mostly positive in tone (warmth, joy, trust), while Gen Z audiences showed a stronger affinity for content expressing nuanced emotional experiences, an incongruity the paper links to low engagement. Formats that ask more of a viewer’s time, such as longer videos and carousels, performed better than static posts.

I was responsible for the project’s analytical work in R, ran the sentiment analysis, turned the results into infographics and animated charts, and wrote this individual paper. The team presented at the 2025 #ScreentimeBU Graduate Student Conference and won the Best Presentation Award.
